### The Direct Answer

There are three order stages in inflatable SUP manufacturing — sample, trial and mass production — and each one de-risks the next. A **sample order** (1–2 boards, ready in 7–12 days) verifies quality and construction. A **trial order** (20–50 boards, production in 25–35 days) validates your market with real sell-through. **Mass production** (90–100+ boards per 150 m fabric roll, also 25–35 days) follows once the format is proven. Choosing the right stage reduces risk and improves cash flow — most new brands move through all three in sequence instead of jumping straight to a container. This guide helps you decide which stage fits your business right now.

### What Is a Sample Order and When to Use It

A sample order is 1–2 boards, usually with your branding applied, built to confirm the product before any financial commitment beyond the sample itself. Typical lead time is **7–12 days** after spec confirmation.

Use a sample order when you are:

- Evaluating a new factory and its workmanship.
- Preparing photos, videos and retailer pitch material.
- Comparing constructions (for example double-layer vs fusion drop-stitch) before choosing.

**Pros:** lowest risk, fastest feedback, minimal capital. **Cons:** highest per-unit cost and never intended for sale. A **sample checklist**: confirm hull rigidity at recommended pressure, stability and capacity, accessory quality (paddle stiffness, pump performance, bag durability) and the accuracy of logo colors on real PVC — screen colors shift on PVC without a Pantone reference.

### What Is a Trial Order and Why It's Ideal for New Brands

A trial order is a small production run with full branding — logo, colors, graphics, accessories and packaging — on proven platforms with stock materials. Typical quantity is **20–50 boards**; production takes **25–35 days** after confirmed PO and deposit. Indicative reference pricing is **USD 180–260 per board (EXW Qingdao)** — higher than volume pricing, but this is real production quality with real QC data at a scale that reflects actual inventory conditions.

Use a trial order when you are:

- Launching your first SUP brand.
- Testing demand in a local market or online store.
- Running a small rental or resort program.

**Pros:** realistic market test, better unit price than samples, complete retail kit. **Cons:** more capital than a sample, and still limited scale. The full landing path is explained in [Launch Your SUP Brand with a Low-MOQ Trial Order](/new-brand-trial-order).

### What Is Mass Production and When to Scale to It

Mass production runs **90–100+ boards per 150 m drop-stitch fabric roll** — or multiple rolls for 300+ board programs, which can ship on staggered schedules. Production takes **25–35 days per batch** after confirmed PO and deposit; custom moulds for bespoke hulls add 15–20 days of tooling beforehand. Indicative reference pricing is **USD 150–220 per board (EXW Qingdao)**.

Move to mass production when you have:

- Validated demand through trial orders or existing sales.
- Multiple retailers or a large rental program to supply.
- A need for stable, repeatable supply across a seasonal peak.

**Pros:** best unit pricing, priority production slots, full customization options (custom Pantone PVC, branded retail packaging). **Cons:** higher capital commitment and a longer planning horizon.

### How to Choose the Right Order Stage for Your Business

Simplified decision path:

- **No sales yet?** → Sample first, then a trial order to test the market.
- **Some sales, not sure about repeat demand?** → Trial order.
- **Consistent monthly sales?** → Mass production; plan reorders ahead of the season.

For a **new brand**, the recommended path is Sample → Trial → Mass Production, starting with 1–2 models rather than many SKUs. For a **distributor or retailer**, start with a trial order to test your market, then move to volume once you have repeat orders. For a **rental or resort operator**, a trial order builds the initial fleet; plan mass production for replacements and expansion before peak season, and coordinate maintenance and repair kits with the factory. ### Common Mistakes When Choosing an Order Stage

- **Jumping straight to mass production without market validation** — the most expensive mistake in SUP sourcing; a trial order buys the same information for a fraction of the capital.
- **Treating sample boards as saleable inventory** — samples are validation units, not stock.
- **Underestimating the time between stages** — feedback, artwork fixes and re-sampling add weeks; plan them into your launch date.
- **Changing too many variables between trial and mass production** — new shape, new construction and new packaging together make the volume run a first-time product again; change one variable per round.

### FAQ

**Can I skip the trial order and go straight to mass production?** Technically yes, but it is risky. Trial orders let you validate demand, pricing and distribution with limited capital. Most new brands benefit from a 20–50 board trial before committing to 100+ boards.

**How many samples should I order before a trial?** Usually 1–2 samples are enough to verify quality and construction. If you are testing multiple constructions, order one sample of each — for example one double-layer and one fusion — rather than several copies of one spec.

**Can I use sample boards for photos and marketing?** Yes. Many brands use sample boards for photoshoots, content creation and retailer pitches before the trial order arrives — sample cost is an investment in go-to-market, not just quality assurance.

**What if my trial order sells out quickly?** That is a strong signal to scale. Place the mass production order while continuing to sell remaining trial stock, and use the sales data to confirm the SKU mix for the volume run.

**Can I get expedited production for a trial or volume order?** Expedited timelines are confirmed against capacity at quotation. Using existing moulds and standard constructions, samples keep the standard 7–12 day window and production stays within the standard 25–35 day window with a priority slot — the trade-off is always capacity, never quality: material curing, 24-hour pressure testing and documentation steps cannot be rushed.
